Stock Market Knowledge Base
1. What Is the Stock Market?
The stock market is a marketplace where investors buy and sell shares of publicly listed companies. A stock represents partial ownership in a company. Stock prices fluctuate based on supply and demand, company performance, economic conditions, investor sentiment, and market news.

2. What Is a Stock?
A stock is a financial security that represents ownership in a company. Investors can potentially earn money through price appreciation and dividends. However, stock investments also carry the risk of losing money.

3. Important Stock Market Terms
Current Price: The latest available trading price of a stock.

Opening Price: The price at which a stock begins trading during a trading session.

Previous Close: The stock's closing price from the previous trading session.

Day High: The highest price reached by a stock during the current trading session.

Day Low: The lowest price reached by a stock during the current trading session.

Market Capitalization: The total market value of a company's outstanding shares. It is calculated by multiplying the current share price by the number of outstanding shares.

Volume: The number of shares traded during a specific period.

Dividend: A portion of a company's profits distributed to eligible shareholders.

P/E Ratio: The price-to-earnings ratio compares a company's share price with its earnings per share. It is commonly used to evaluate stock valuation.

EPS: Earnings per share represents the portion of a company's profit allocated to each outstanding share.

4. Types of Stock Analysis
Fundamental Analysis
Fundamental analysis evaluates a company's financial health, business performance, profitability, revenue growth, debt, and valuation. Investors use it to estimate the long-term value of a company.

Important metrics include revenue, net profit, earnings per share, P/E ratio, debt-to-equity ratio, and dividend yield.

Technical Analysis
Technical analysis studies historical price movements, trading volume, and chart patterns to identify possible future price movements.

Common indicators include the Relative Strength Index (RSI), Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD), 50-day moving average, and 200-day moving average.

Sentiment Analysis
Sentiment analysis evaluates market participants' attitudes toward a stock using news, company announcements, analyst opinions, and investor discussions. Positive sentiment may support buying interest, while negative sentiment may increase selling pressure.

5. Stock Market Trends
Bullish Trend: A market condition in which prices generally move upward, indicating increasing buying interest.

Bearish Trend: A market condition in which prices generally move downward, indicating selling pressure.

Sideways Trend: A market condition in which prices move within a relatively narrow range without a clear upward or downward direction.

A trend does not guarantee future price movement.

6. Stock Recommendations
Buy: An assessment that a stock may offer an attractive investment opportunity based on the available analysis.

Hold: An assessment that maintaining an existing position may be reasonable, depending on the investor's goals and risk tolerance.

Sell: An assessment that reducing or closing a position may be appropriate based on the analysis.

Strong Buy: A particularly positive assessment supported by multiple favorable factors. This is not a guarantee of profit.

Strong Sell: A particularly negative assessment suggesting significant concerns about the stock's outlook.

Recommendations should consider valuation, business fundamentals, market conditions, investment horizon, and risk tolerance.

7. Risk Management
Risk management helps investors control potential losses and make informed investment decisions.

Stop Loss: A predetermined price level at which an investor intends to exit a position to limit losses. Execution price may differ from the specified level.

Target Price: An estimated future price based on an analysis or valuation method. It is not a guaranteed outcome.

Risk-Reward Ratio: A comparison between the potential loss and potential profit of a trade. For example, risking ₹10 to potentially earn ₹20 gives a potential risk-reward ratio of 1:2.

Diversification: Spreading investments across different companies, sectors, or asset classes to reduce concentration risk.

Volatility: The degree to which a stock's price fluctuates over time. Higher volatility can mean larger price movements in either direction.

8. Investment Time Horizons
Intraday Trading: Buying and selling stocks within the same trading day.

Short-Term Investing: Holding stocks for a relatively short period, such as several days, weeks, or months.

Long-Term Investing: Holding stocks for multiple years to potentially benefit from business growth and long-term value creation.

Different time horizons require different analysis methods and risk-management approaches.

9. Fundamental Financial Metrics
Revenue Growth: The percentage increase or decrease in a company's revenue over a specific period.

Profit Margin: The percentage of revenue remaining as profit after accounting for relevant expenses.

Debt-to-Equity Ratio: A measure comparing a company's debt with shareholders' equity. Interpretation varies across industries.

Free Cash Flow: Cash generated by a company after accounting for capital expenditures. It can help assess financial flexibility.

Return on Equity (ROE): A measure of how efficiently a company generates profit relative to shareholders' equity.

No single financial metric is sufficient to determine whether a stock is a good investment.

10. Technical Indicators
Relative Strength Index (RSI): A momentum indicator ranging from 0 to 100. Values above 70 are commonly considered overbought, while values below 30 are commonly considered oversold. These levels do not automatically signal a reversal.

Moving Average: The average stock price over a specified period, often used to identify trends.

MACD: An indicator that compares moving averages to assess momentum and possible trend changes.

Support Level: A price area where buying interest has historically emerged.

Resistance Level: A price area where selling pressure has historically emerged.

Support and resistance levels are estimates, not guaranteed price barriers.

11. Factors That Affect Stock Prices
Stock prices can change due to company earnings, revenue growth, management decisions, industry competition, interest rates, inflation, government policies, global economic events, geopolitical developments, and investor sentiment.

Unexpected news and changes in market liquidity can also cause significant price movements.

12. Stock Analysis Process
A systematic stock analysis can follow these steps:

Identify the company, ticker symbol, and stock exchange.

Obtain the latest available market price and verify its timestamp.

Evaluate the company's financial performance and valuation.

Review price trends and relevant technical indicators.

Identify positive factors, negative factors, and key risks.

Consider the investment horizon and investor risk tolerance.

Estimate a target price and potential downside, where appropriate.

Formulate a reasoned buy, hold, or sell assessment.

Explain the conclusion and its uncertainties.

14. Reliable Stock Data
Stock analysis should use reliable and up-to-date data sources. Market prices, trading volume, financial statements, and corporate announcements should be verified using exchange publications, company investor-relations pages, or reputable financial-data providers.

Every price should ideally include its currency, source, timestamp, and applicable exchange. Historical prices should not be presented as current prices.

An AI model should not invent missing prices, financial ratios, or company information. When reliable data is unavailable, it should explicitly state that the information could not be verified.

15. Limitations of Stock Predictions
Stock analysis involves uncertainty. Historical performance does not guarantee future returns, technical indicators can produce false signals, and financial statements may not reflect future business conditions.

AI-generated analysis can contain errors or outdated information. Investors should independently verify important figures and consider their financial objectives and risk tolerance before making investment decisions.
